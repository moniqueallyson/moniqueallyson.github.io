Background

What is the Future of Food Security?

Marine aquaculture has the potential to be a more sustainable option to create future food security than the alternative land-based approach as the rising human population is estimated to reach 10 billion by 2050 (Gentry et al, 2017). Traditional farming is highly resource intensive. Beef production uses 61.1kg of grain and 28 kg for pork production, while 1 kg of finfish would use less than 13.5 kg of grain (Hall et al. 2011). Despite the promising solution, there is very little known about the areas on where species can thrive and aquacultures can succeed. Gentry et al. calculated the growth performance index and spatially assessed the location for general growing potential for suitable fish and bivalves (2017).

Why the Ocean?

The Earth’s living space is represented by 99% of water by volume and the ocean contains 97%. For this reason, the United Nations has implemented a Sustainable Development Goal 14 (Life Below Water) to include ocean conservation and encourage marine resources to be utilized efficiently (2022). This also ties into other United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals 1 (No poverty) and 2 (Zero hunger). Marine aquaculture seeks to meet the protein demands with the ocean representing an opportunity for food production with its potential being regarded as mostly untapped in terms of a farming source(Gentry et al. 2017).

Research Question

This analysis determines the suitable locations along the west coast for several oyster species and blue mussel using Exclusive Economic Zones(EEZ). EEZ is an area that extends roughly 230 miles beyond a nation’s territorial sea which is a boundary on where costal nations have jurisdiction over all resources, living and non-living (NOAA, 2020).

Suitable growing conditions are dependent on sea surface temperature and ocean depth for species’ optimal growth.

Species Depth Sea Surface Temperature
Oyster 0 - 70 meters below 11- 30 °C
Blue Mussel 0 - 60 meters below 5 - 20 °C

Blue mussels, Mytilus edulis, are eurythermal and well adapted to temperatures of 5–20 °C, with an upper thermal tolerance limit of about 29 °C for adults (Matoo et al. 2021). The depth for blue mussels was found on the website SeaLifeBase and the oyster’s information was given by the instructor.

# single raster of average SST
sst_mean_c <- mean(sst, na.rm = TRUE) - 273.15

Depth and SST had different extents, resolutions, and origins, which were redefined using resample() and crop() for proper alignment.

Two different CRS between the three datasets:

  • depth: EPSG 4326
  • sst: EPSG 9122
  • eez_data: EPSG 9122

Since there were two datasets with the same CRS, the choice to change depth to match the others was decided.

Show code 
# Crop data to match SST raster
crop_depth <- crop(depth, sst_mean_c)

# Transfer sst data using bilear method to match
resampled_depth <- resample(crop_depth, sst_mean_c, method = "bilinear")

# test if the resampled depth CRS matches the sst_mean_c CRS
  if (crs(resampled_depth) == crs(sst_mean_c)) {
    print("CRS match!")
  } else {
    warning("CRS don't match!")
    crs(resampled_depth) <- crs(sst_mean_c)
  }

Suitable location workflow

The following five steps are to build a function that will be used later to find suitable locations for marine species based on their sst and depth requirements.

Step 1. Reclassifying based on conditions

Both sst and depth were reclassified into 0’s and 1’s coinciding suitability of locations by creating a reclassification matrix.

Rows in the matrix are as following:

  • outside the lower range receive 0
  • suitable condition within range receives 1
  • outside the higher range recieve 0
Show code 
# Variables for min and max SST
min_sst <- 11
max_sst <- 30

# SST reclassification matrix
  rcl_sst <- matrix(
    c(
      -Inf, min_sst, 0,
      min_sst, max_sst, 1,
      max_sst, Inf, 0
    ),
    ncol = 3,
    byrow = TRUE
  )

# Variables for min and max depth
min_depth <- 0
max_depth <- -70

# Depth reclassification matrix
rcl_depth <- matrix(
    c(
      -Inf, max_depth, 0,
      max_depth, min_depth, 1,
      min_depth, Inf, 0
    ),
    ncol = 3,
    byrow = TRUE
  )

# Reclassify sst and depth to suitable zones
zone_sst <- classify(sst_mean_c, rcl = rcl_sst)

zone_depth <- classify(resampled_depth, rcl = rcl_depth)

# Combine to adhere to satisfy both sst and depth for suitable zones
depth_sst_zones <- c(zone_sst, zone_depth)

Step 2. Combine with map algebra

After reclassification, a function for map alegbra using lapp() to layers as arguments.

Show code 
zone_fun <- function(sst, depth){
  sst * depth
}
# Find suitable zones for oysters adhering to both conditions
suitable_zones <- lapp(depth_sst_zones,
 fun = zone_fun)

Step 4. Calculate the suitable areas within each EEZ region

Before moving onto any other spatial operations, cellSize() was used to compute the true area (km^2) of each cell to determine the suitable locations within EEZ.

Show code 
# Calculate cell size
cell_size <- cellSize(suitable_zones, unit = "km", mask = TRUE)

Then set values to 0 that are outside the boundary and use only cells with value 1 to determing the suitable locations using mask().

Show code 
# Use only the cells with value 1 as suitable in the total area
suitable_zmask <- mask(cell_size,suitable_zones, maskvalues = 0)

Use the zonal() operation to apply the sum of area using the EEZ raster and the masked suitable zones.

Show code 
# Select raster cells by suitable zpmes inside EEZ
desired_zone <- zonal(suitable_zmask,eez_raster, fun = 'sum', na.rm = TRUE)

Function: Find Suitable Area for Marine Species

The function is called after EEZ species and used as shown:

eez_species(min_sst, max_sst, min_depth, max_depth, species_name)

The function utilizes the five steps detailed above and creates a map or a table for the given species by inputting the following:

  • minimum and maximum SST
  • minimum and maximum depth
  • “species name”
Show code 
eez_species <- function(
    min_sst,
    max_sst,
    min_depth,
    max_depth,
    species_name
){
# Step 1. SST matrix reclassification
    rcl_sst <- matrix(
    c(
      -Inf, min_sst, 0,
      min_sst, max_sst, 1,
      max_sst, Inf, 0
    ),
    ncol = 3,
    byrow = TRUE
    )

# Reclassify SST
    zone_sst <- classify(sst_mean_c, rcl = rcl_sst)
    
# Reclassify depth
    rcl_depth <- matrix(
    c(
      -Inf, max_depth, 0,
      max_depth, min_depth, 1,
      min_depth, Inf, 0
    ),
    ncol = 3,
    byrow = TRUE
    )

    zone_depth <- classify(resampled_depth, rcl = rcl_depth)

# Combine the two zones together 
    depth_sst_zones <- c(zone_sst, zone_depth)
    
# Step 2. Define function 
zone_fun <- function(sst, depth){
  sst * depth}

# Find suitable zones
    suitable_zones <- lapp(depth_sst_zones, 
    fun = zone_fun)
    
# Step 3. Convert to raster
    eez_raster <- rasterize(eez_data, suitable_zones, field = "rgn_id")
    
# Step 4. Calculate cell size
    cell_size <- cellSize(suitable_zones, unit = "km", mask = TRUE)
    
# Keep cell area only where suitable areas equals 1

    suitable_zmask <- mask(cell_size,suitable_zones, maskvalues = 0)
    
# Use zonal function to sum masked result and eez raster

    desired_zone <- zonal(suitable_zmask,eez_raster, fun = 'sum', na.rm = TRUE)
    
# Step 5. Create tibble
    z_tibble <- as_tibble(desired_zone)
    
# Merge for desired EEZ regions
    map <- left_join(eez_data, z_tibble, by = "rgn_id")

# Create color palette   
   color_palette <- c("#d0f0f0", "#5fb3c7", "#1f78a8", "#08306b")

# Visualize EEZ
   map_species <- tm_basemap("OpenStreetMap") +
  tm_shape(map) +
  tm_polygons(
    fill = "area",
    fill_alpha = 0.85,
    col = "grey30",
    fill.scale = tm_scale_continuous(values = color_palette),
    fill.legend = tm_legend(
      title = "Suitable Area (km²)",
      position = tm_pos_out("right", "center")
    )
  ) +
  tm_text(text = "rgn_key", size = 0.7, col = "grey20") +
  tm_compass(type = "8star", size = 2, position = c("right", "top")) +
  tm_scalebar(position = c("left", "bottom")) +
  tm_options(component.autoscale = FALSE) +
  tm_title(paste0("Suitable ", species_name,
  " Areas by EEZ in the West Coast"), size = 1)

# Create a table

table <- map |> 
  select(rgn_id, area, area_km2) |>
  arrange(desc(area)) |>
  mutate(pct_suitable = area / area_km2 * 100) |>
  st_drop_geometry()

return(list(map_species, table))

}

Discussion

The different requirements per species resulted in different suitable geographic regions on the West Coast. The blue mussels are inclined to colder waters, such as those found in Washingoton, while oysters are inclined to slightly warmer waters, from Central to Southern California, for optimal growth. A limitation that may impact these results can come from the sea surface temperature data ranging from 2008 to 2012, which may need to be expanded in order to capture the patterns of annual or seasonal changes, expecially within the 2020’s as there have been numerous record breaking temperatures. For blue mussels, warming waters affects growth not ocean acidification (OA) though the study was limited to adults excluding larval stage (Matoo et al. 2021). OA may not be a factor into blue mussel growth, but there could be others that could increase or decrease the suitable zones beyond temperature and depth. The ocean can be limited by national jurisdiction by the military or even energy production which reduces the available space for aquaculture (Gentry et al. 2017).

Future direction

  1. Demands: High demands on resources can intensify animal farming and inputs such as energy or ecosystem services like nutrient cycling (Hall et al. 2011).
  2. Development: To further understand future development of marine aquaculture across the world, research and policy development is needed on the integration of growth potential with economic and social aspects that coincide with aquaculture (Gentry et al. 2017).
  3. Consideration: Several communities are dependent on traditional fisheries and would benefit from consideration by integrating social, political, cultural, ecological dimensions into aquaculture planning where policymakers can practice expansion rather than replacement and support an equitable and sustainable seafood future ().
